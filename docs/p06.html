Portfolio 6: Simulating Parenting and Civic Engagement Data

Overview:

Goal:

I want to use my portfolio to share some of the analytic work I did for my Master’s thesis. However, I need to keep my data private. My goal for this piece is to simulate a dataset that resembles my thesis data. I plan to use this simulated dataset in future portfolio pieces.

Data:

My original dataset contained youth-reported measures of parenting practices, civic engagement, and demographic information collected over three waves of a longitudinal study (labeled here as Times 1-3). Participants made up 11 different cultural/geographic groups, which were represented by effect-coded predictors in my final regression models. There was quite a bit of missing data across waves, so to simplify my simulation, I will use the most restricted subsample I had (668 participants).

Specific core variables include:

  • Joint decision making at Time 1 (JDM; a measure of moderate support for autonomy)
  • Independent decision making at Time 1 (IDM; a measure of extreme support for autonomy)
  • Maternal warmth at Time 1 (MatWarmth)
  • Paternal warmth at Time 1 (PatWarmth)
  • Civic engagement at Time 2 (CE_2)
  • Civic engagement at Time 3 (CE_3)

Recruitment groups include:

  • Naples, Italy
  • Rome, Italy
  • Kenya
  • Philippines
  • Thailand
  • Sweden
  • US: African American
  • US: European American
  • US: Hispanic
  • Colombia
  • Jordan

Product:

A simulated dataset containing all variables necessary for the analyses I completed for my thesis. (I obtained all parameter values by running descriptive and correlational analyses with the original dataset in SPSS.)

Interpretation:

The simulation worked well for recreating my data! The biggest challenge was figuring out how to model (1) group differences in the average levels of the core variables and (2) correlations among the core variables at the same time. I ended up simulating the variables based on their correlations with each other first, and then adding “rnorm” terms by group to reflect group differences. This allowed me to model the main effects of the grouping variable on civic engagement. However, I did not model any interactions between group membership and the parenting variables. To further develop my simulation skills, I plan to simulate data with two-way interactions in a future portfolio piece!

One thing missing from my simulation is that I did not account for any differences between the recruitment groups. This is not a problem for the regressions that I want to run in my next portfolio piece (which use the whole sample without accounting for group differences). However, for part of my thesis, I examined group differences in civic engagement to see if trends in civic engagement corresponded to country-level income inequality. To account for this in my simulated data, I need to adjust my civic engagement variables to vary by group.

This would get kind of complicated with all 11 groups, so to simplify my second simulation, I will just include two groups. Because I was interested in examining civic engagement in countries with different levels of income inequality, I will use the country with the highest inequality (Colombia) and the country with the lowest inequality (Sweden) according to estimates from the World Inequality Database.

Now, I need to create variation among the groups. I am mainly interested in how levels of civic engagement differ across countries with different levels of socioeconomic inequality, so I will focus on varying the two measures of civic engagement (reports at Time 2 and Time 3).

I am setting the means in rnorm to be the amount above/below the overall sample mean that I want to shift the mean for each group. Civic engagement was lower in Sweden and higher in Colombia.
